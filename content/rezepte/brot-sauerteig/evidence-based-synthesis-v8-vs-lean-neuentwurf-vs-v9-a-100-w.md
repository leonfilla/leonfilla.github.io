## TL;DR
- **The Lean Neuentwurf wins more design decisions than V8**, but neither is optimal: the V9 below keeps the Lean recipe's structural backbone (rye-driven phytase, mild lactic fermentation, full machine kneading, vital wheat gluten, no tangzhong, no psyllium, no nutritional yeast, no molasses) and adds **one evidence-supported upgrade — a 45 °C salt-free phytase activation soak of the whole-grain flours before levain addition** — projected to push phytate degradation comfortably above 80%.
- **Phytate degradation >80% is achievable** with the recipe below: rye contributes ~2.3× the endogenous phytase of wheat (Reale et al., *J. Agric. Food Chem.* 2007, 55:2993–2997: "rye, 6750 mU g⁻¹; wheat, 2930 mU g⁻¹; oat, 23 mU g⁻¹"), sourdough pH 4.5–5.0 keeps that phytase near its optimum, and a 45–55 °C pre-soak adds the largest single boost reported in the literature (~80% PA hydrolysis in 2 h of wheat meal at 55 °C, Türk & Sandberg, *Cereal Chem.* 1992).
- **For free-standing open crumb in 100 % whole grain, the dominant levers are (in order):** (1) fine but not ultra-fine bran (medium-fine wheat, finely milled rye), (2) full machine gluten development with 4 % added vital wheat gluten, (3) 75–78 % hydration (higher spreads on a stone without a Dutch oven), (4) single gentle shaping + cold retard + bake-from-cold, (5) stone + active steam in the Bosch HNG978NB1. Tangzhong, psyllium, flax, lentil flour and pumpkin seed flour each hurt at least one of these levers.

## Key Findings

### 1. Phytate degradation — the achievable ceiling and how to reach it
- **Plain whole-wheat sourdough alone reaches ~62 % phytate degradation; plain yeast-leavened whole-wheat bread ~38 %** (Leenhardt et al., *J. Agric. Food Chem.* 2005, 53:98–102; Lopez et al. 2001). With prolonged sourdough fermentation and bran pre-incubation, degradation approaches ~90 %.
- **Rye sourdough reaches essentially complete phytate degradation** because rye has the highest endogenous phytase among temperate cereals. Reale et al. (*J. Agric. Food Chem.* 2007, 55:2993–2997, doi:10.1021/jf063507n) directly compared cereal flours under sourdough fermentation: *"Lactic acid fermentation of cereal flours resulted in a 100 (rye), 95−100 (wheat), and 39−47% (oat) reduction in phytate content within 24 h … positively correlated with endogenous plant phytase activity (rye, 6750 mU g⁻¹; wheat, 2930 mU g⁻¹; oat, 23 mU g⁻¹)."* Pontoppidan et al. (*Anim. Feed Sci. Technol.* 2012, 177:227–236) report 3.14 vs 1.77 FTU/g for rye vs wheat reference activity.
- **pH optimum of cereal phytase is 5.0–5.6** (wheat 5.0–5.6, rye 5.5–6.0, barley 5.0; Brinch-Pedersen et al. 2002); **temperature optimum 45–55 °C**. Activity at sourdough pH (~4.5) drops to roughly 20 % of optimum but is still meaningful.
- **A moderate pH drop to 5.5 already triggers ~70 % phytate breakdown** in wheat dough vs 40 % with no acidification (Leenhardt et al. 2005). This is the single highest-leverage step in the entire workflow.
- **A 55 °C hydrothermal hold of wheat meal hydrolyses ~80 % of phytate in 2 h and >95 % in 4 h** (Türk & Sandberg, *Cereal Chem.* 1992, 69:266). Above 55 °C, hydrolysis declines because phytase begins to inactivate.
- **Sourdough fermentation temperature: 25 °C beats 30 °C and 35 °C** for phytate degradation in 100 % whole-wheat sourdough. Yıldırım & Arıcı (*LWT* 2019, 112:108224, doi:10.1016/j.lwt.2019.05.122) found: *"Lactobacillus brevis HEB33 exhibited the highest phytase activity at 803.95 U/mL. Optimal fermentation temperature for phytic acid degradation was 25 °C, achieving 69.76 % degradation."* 35 °C also damaged bread quality and sensory scores. This is at odds with the lactic/acetic temperature preference (warmer = more lactic = milder) — see trade-off in Recommendations.
- **Salt does not meaningfully inhibit endogenous cereal phytase at dough-relevant concentrations.** Sedaghati et al. (*J. Agric. Sci. Technol.* 2011, 13:1065–1076) report that *"fermentation … and salt addition (0 to 1.5%) to the dough samples resulted in an increased phytase activity"* in three Iranian wheat cultivars. The "salt inhibits phytase" claim originates from microbial phytase studies (*L. sanfranciscensis*; De Angelis et al., *Int. J. Food Microbiol.* 2003) and is weaker for the endogenous wheat/rye enzyme that does most of the work in real bread. → **The salt-free warm autolyse is justified primarily by phytase pH/temperature optimum, not by salt inhibition; keeping it salt-free is cheap insurance but not a load-bearing rationale.**
- **Tangzhong (65 °C) destroys most phytase in the scalded fraction.** Cereal phytases are stable to ~50–55 °C but lose substantial activity at 60–65 °C and ≥80 % activity at 70–80 °C (Pontoppidan et al. 2012). Scalding only ~7 % of total flour is not catastrophic, but it is a structural negative because the scalded fraction is the most bran-rich in a whole-grain recipe.

### 2. Net phytate budget of V8's "functional" ingredients
Published phytic-acid contents (mg/g dry matter):
- Whole wheat flour: ~6–10 mg/g (García-Estepa et al., *Food Res. Int.* 1999, 32:217–221)
- Whole rye flour: ~4.5–7.5 mg/g (García-Estepa 1999)
- Red lentil flour (dehulled): ~6–11 mg/g (Aguilera et al. 2014; legume reviews)
- Pumpkin seed kernel (*Cucurbita pepo*): ~4–6 mg/g (Mansour et al., *J. Sci. Food Agric.* 1993, 61:73–78)
- **Hulled hemp hearts: ~22 mg/g (whole hemp 43–75 mg/g)** (Galasso et al., *Front. Plant Sci.* 2016, 7:688; Multari et al., *Food Chem.* 2022, doi:10.1016/j.foodchem.2021.131718)
- **Brown flaxseed: 23–33 mg/g** (Oomah et al., *J. Agric. Food Chem.* 1996, 44:2663–2666, doi:10.1021/jf9601527)

**Implication:** V8 layers four high-phytate non-cereal ingredients (lentil, pumpkin seed, hemp, flax) on top of the cereal grain. Hemp and flax in particular load 2–5× the per-gram phytate of whole wheat. Even at 80 % cereal-driven degradation, the *added* phytate from 30 g flaxseed + 25 g hemp seeds + 75 g lentil flour + 25 g pumpkin seed flour produces a meaningful residual phytate burden that the cereal phytase cannot reach efficiently (phytase activity in lentils, flax, hemp, and pumpkin seed is far lower than in rye/wheat). The Lean Neuentwurf is correct to remove three of these and keep only hulled hemp at a token 6 %; even that load is not trivial. **V9 reduces hemp further and removes flax and lentil entirely.**

### 3. Open crumb in 100 % whole-grain free-standing loaves — the actual controlling factors

**Bran particle size — equivocal, with an inverted-U optimum.** Noort et al. (*J. Cereal Sci.* 2010, 52:59) found that *reducing* bran particle size made bread worse, attributed to increased fibre–protein interaction surface and liberation of reactive components (e.g., glutathione, ferulic acid) that disrupt the gluten network. However, durum-wheat bran work (Cai et al. 2014; PMC7996265) found that medium-fine bran (~300 µm) outperforms both coarse (>600 µm) and ultra-fine (<180 µm) bran. **Practical conclusion:** "finely milled, fine whole grain" is fine; "stone-milled coarse" probably *also* fine; "ultra-fine sifted bran" is worst. The user's "strong, fine whole wheat" choice is consistent with the best-evidence band.

**Vital wheat gluten — modest but real volume gain.** America's Test Kitchen reports ~½-inch additional rise in a hearty whole-wheat loaf with VWG at the manufacturer's 1½ tsp per cup of flour (~3 %). Giannou & Tzia (*Foods* 2016, 5:6) tested 2/4/5/6 % VWG in whole-wheat dough and found 4 % an inflection point: dough strength and frozen-stability gains plateau or reverse above ~5 %. **V9 settles on 4 %** — enough to compensate the bran's gluten dilution, not so much that the crumb becomes rubbery.

**Hydration — 75–78 % is the sweet spot for free-standing whole-grain loaves.** Forkish's *Flour Water Salt Yeast* and the King Arthur hydration guide converge on the rule that **whole wheat requires ~5–7 % higher hydration than white flour for equivalent crumb softness**, but free-standing loaves *spread* above ~80 % without a Dutch oven. The user's 85 % V8 is too high for a stone bake; the Lean 78 % is on-target. **V9 uses 78 % effective hydration** (calculated on grain flour + VWG; hemp not in the flour percentage).

**Vital wheat gluten + machine knead replaces stretch-and-fold without penalty.** No peer-reviewed study shows S&F outperforms full machine development on a controlled basis; bakery practice converges that both work, with machine kneading being faster and more reproducible. Given the user's hard constraint (machine-only, minimal handling), this is decisive. The standard rule (window-pane test on a spiral hook) is the right reproducibility metric; no timed S&F sets needed.

**Tangzhong is the wrong tool for a free-standing, crispy-crusted, open-crumb loaf.** Tangzhong is a *soft-bread* technique (shokupan, Hokkaido milk bread, soft cinnamon rolls) where the goal is moisture retention, cake-like softness, and shelf life (King Arthur Baking; America's Test Kitchen). Reviewers consistently report tangzhong dough is "sticky and weak with AP flour" and produces a "cake-like" crumb. In a 100 % whole-grain lean hearth loaf, these effects work *against* both the open crumb (gelatinised starch dilutes the protein matrix in the scalded fraction) and the crispy crust (the loaf retains more moisture and crust softens faster). Combined with the phytase-destruction issue at 65 °C, **V9 drops the tangzhong**.

**Psyllium husk — positive in gluten-free / very-high-bran systems, neutral-to-negative in a 100 % whole-wheat sourdough with adequate gluten and added VWG.** Stankevičienė et al. (Frontiers in Nutrition 2023, PMC10641513) show that 5–10 % psyllium husk gel *increases* specific volume and porosity of wheat bread by reinforcing the viscoelastic network. But at >15 % the crumb becomes less uniform; and these gains are largest in gluten-deficient systems. In a 100 % whole-grain wheat-dominated dough with 4 % VWG and a well-fermented levain, psyllium is redundant; V8's 1 % psyllium is below the threshold where the volume gain is statistically detectable in wheat-flour studies. **V9 omits psyllium** — minor sensory loss (slightly less moist crumb), zero open-crumb loss.

**Ground flaxseed at 6 % barely hurts loaf volume but adds significant phytate.** Xu, Hall & Wolf-Hall (*J. Food Res.* 2014, 3:83) report that 6 and 10 % flaxseed flour do not significantly change specific volume vs control, though oven spring is reduced. Koca & Anıl (*JSFA* 2007, 87:1172, doi:10.1002/jsfa.2739) actually found 10 % flax *increased* loaf volume and delayed staling. So the open-crumb case against flax is weak — but the phytate case is decisive (23–33 mg/g flax × 30 g = ~700–1,000 mg added phytate, which the residual cereal phytase cannot reach with high efficiency because flax phytase activity is negligible). **V9 omits flax.**

**Red lentil flour at 15 % significantly reduces loaf volume.** Marchini et al. (*LWT* 2021, 139:110523, doi:10.1016/j.lwt.2020.110523) and Mohammed et al. report specific-volume reductions of ~15–32 % at 15–20 % substitution, with crumb hardness rising 40–250 %. Codină et al. (*Gels* 2025, 11:894) report dough max height collapsing from 53.8 mm (control) to <35 mm above 10 % lentil. Combined with the lysine-irrelevance argument (milk toppings cover lysine; PDCAAS irrelevant), **V9 omits lentil flour**. The Lean Neuentwurf decision is solid.

**Pumpkin seed flour at 5 % — small volume penalty, redundant minerals.** Pumpkin seed flour adds ~4–6 mg/g phytate of its own. Hulled hemp covers zinc more cleanly. V9 omits.

**Cold retard and bake-from-cold.** Cold dough scores cleanly, holds shape better, and produces oven spring at least as good as room-temperature dough — the standard finding across artisan bakers. Beyond ~48 h, protease activity degrades the gluten network. **V9 uses 14–18 h cold retard and bakes directly from the fridge.**

**Stone + active steam vs Dutch oven.** With the Bosch HNG978NB1's 3-level steam injection and 300 °C top/bottom heat, a stone bake can match Dutch-oven crust and spring — the dominant factor is *steam delivery and retention* in the first 10–15 min, not vessel geometry. The Bosch steam oven is purpose-built for this; V9 leverages it.

### 4. Mild flavour: lactic-dominant fermentation
De Vuyst, Comasio & Van Kerrebroeck (*Crit. Rev. Food Sci. Nutr.* 2023, 63:2447–2479) state that the desirable lactic-to-acetic ratio in sourdough is "*preferably around 3.0–5.0 moles of lactic acid per mole of acetic acid*." Warmer (~28–35 °C) and wetter dough shifts the fermentation quotient toward lactic-dominant; cold (<22 °C) and dry doughs increase acetic. Stopping bulk at pH 4.5–4.8 (not 4.0) keeps total titratable acidity moderate. **V9 ferments at 28 °C in bulk for mildness, and uses a 45 °C salt-free pre-ferment soak (NOT a fermentation step — an enzymatic activation step before the levain is added) to harvest the phytate-degradation gains.** This decouples the temperature trade-off: phytase gets its 45 °C window separately from fermentation flavour management.

### 5. Glycemic index, prebiotics, iron, and B-vitamins
- **Sourdough lowers GI to ~54 vs ~71 for whole-wheat yeast bread.** The 2024 systematic review by Rolim et al. (*Crit. Rev. Food Sci. Nutr.* 64:801–816, doi:10.1080/10408398.2022.2108756) analysed 18 clinical trials and concluded "*the consumption of sourdough bread has a lower impact on blood glucose*," with a mean difference of −0.29 mmol/L (−5.2 mg/dL) at the 60-minute postprandial mark vs industrially fermented bread. Mechanisms: lactic acid slows starch hydrolysis, acetic acid slows gastric emptying, increased resistant starch on cooling. Sourdough alone is the dominant GI lever — more important than psyllium, flax, or molasses.
- **Arabinoxylans (rye, wheat bran) are robust prebiotics** that selectively support *Bifidobacterium* and *Lactobacillus* and produce butyrate, propionate, acetate (Demuth et al., *Front. Nutr.* 2021, 8:707763). Rye-derived AX ferments more slowly than wheat-bran AX — useful for distal-colon SCFA delivery. V9's 15 % rye plus whole-wheat bran provides ample AX without extra psyllium.
- **Molasses iron is non-heme (~3.5 mg/Tbsp, ~7 mg/Tbsp blackstrap)** with low absorption that is *further depressed* by the bread's residual phytate. Christides & Sharp (*PLoS ONE* 2013, PMC3858368) show fructose can enhance non-heme iron uptake in Caco-2 cells, but the effect is fully reversed by phytic acid. Molasses is mostly free sugar (glycemic load ~55 per reference table) for marginal iron payoff. **The Lean rejection is well-supported. V9 omits molasses.**
- **Nutritional yeast** is a real source of B-vitamins (and often B12 when fortified), but it (a) adds glutamate/umami flavour the user explicitly does not want, and (b) provides protein quality benefits irrelevant given the milk-topping context. For an omnivore eating dairy, B12 sufficiency is not at risk. **V9 omits.**

## V9 — The Synthesized Recipe

**Total flour basis = 500 g grain flour. Baker's % on grain flour (VWG counted in flour total; hemp NOT counted).**

### Ingredients
- Whole wheat flour, strong, finely milled — **425 g (85.0 %)**
- Whole rye flour, fine — **75 g (15.0 %)**
- Vital wheat gluten — **20 g (4.0 %)**
- Hulled hemp seeds (texture-neutral) — **20 g (4.0 %)** *not in flour total*
- Water (total in dough) — **390 g (78.0 %)**
- Fine salt — **11 g (2.2 %)**
- Mature liquid sourdough levain (see below) — **100 g (20.0 %)**

**Starter / Levain build (night before bake morning):**
- From the user's fridge-stored whole-wheat starter (last fed 7 d), build a 1 : 5 : 5 levain:
  - 10 g starter + 50 g whole-wheat flour + 50 g water (28 °C).
  - Ferment 8–10 h at 26–28 °C until pH ≈ 4.2 and bubbly across the surface; use immediately. Discard surplus.

### Workflow (machine-only, minimal handling, free-standing, controlled by pH endpoints)

**Stage 1 — 45 °C salt-free phytase activation soak (the load-bearing nutritional step)**
- In the Wilfa ProBaker bowl: combine **whole wheat 425 g + rye 75 g + water 380 g** (10 g of the 390 g water held back for the salt/levain stage to avoid splash).
- Mix with K-hook on low for 90 s until homogeneous, no dry flour. **Do NOT add salt, VWG, levain or hemp.**
- Cover. Place in the Instant Pot Pro Max set to sous-vide mode at **45 °C** (water bath around an inset bowl, or use the IP yogurt/sous-vide mode with the dough bowl seated on a trivet over hot water — goal: *dough-mass temperature 45 ± 2 °C*).
- Hold **45 min** at 45 °C. The flour itself drops the slurry to pH ~5.8–6.2 — within the rye/wheat phytase optimum.
- *Why this works:* 45 °C is in the rye-phytase temperature optimum (45–55 °C) and within the wheat-phytase optimum; pH 5.8–6.2 is at the cereal phytase pH optimum; absence of salt removes any residual concern about inhibition. Based on Türk & Sandberg 1992, this 45-min head start at 45 °C is expected to hydrolyse roughly 40–60 % of the phytate *before* the levain is even added.

**Stage 2 — Cool, add levain + salt + VWG + hemp, machine-develop the gluten**
- Move bowl to room temperature. Add **levain 100 g + salt 11 g + VWG 20 g + hulled hemp 20 g + remaining 10 g water**. Dough temperature target after mixing: **27–28 °C**.
- Switch to spiral hook. Mix on speed 1 for 4 min to incorporate, then speed 2 for 5–7 min until full window-pane (dough stretches translucent without tearing; strong, cohesive, slightly tacky mass). Total mix after levain: ~10–12 min.

**Stage 3 — Bulk fermentation, pH-controlled**
- Place in a straight-sided container; mark start volume.
- Bulk at **28 °C** (Instant Pot proofer mode at 28 °C, or warm spot). **No stretch-and-fold.**
- End point: dough has risen **~60–70 %** (not doubled) AND surface pH measures **4.55–4.65** with the Apera PH60F (insert tip ~1 cm into the dough surface). Expected time: ~3.5–4.5 h.
- *Why 28 °C and pH 4.6:* warm enough to favour lactic acid (lactic:acetic ~3–5 target per De Vuyst et al. 2023 → mild flavour); 78 % hydration keeps LAB in the lactic-dominant band; stopping at pH 4.6 (not 4.2) caps total titratable acidity so the loaf does not taste sharp.

**Stage 4 — Single shape (minimal contact)**
- Lightly flour the bench. Turn dough out. **One** gentle round-up with a bench scraper — no pre-shape, no S&F, no aggressive de-gas. Aim for one lap of surface tension. Transfer immediately seam-up to a floured banneton or rye-floured linen-lined basket.
- Cover with a shower cap.

**Stage 5 — Cold retard, 14–18 h at 4 °C**
- Refrigerate at **4 °C for 14–18 h** (overnight). Beyond 24 h, gluten degrades. Below 14 h, scoring and shape suffer.
- Continued slow phytase + LAB activity during retard adds another increment of phytate degradation.

**Stage 6 — Bake from cold, stone + 3-level steam (Bosch HNG978NB1)**
- Preheat Bosch oven with the pizza stone on the middle shelf to **250 °C top/bottom heat** (no convection). Stone soak ≥ 45 min after the oven beep.
- Take the dough straight from the fridge, invert onto parchment, score (single decisive slash, blade at ~30 °). Slide parchment + dough onto stone.
- **Steam profile:**
  - 0–15 min: 250 °C, **steam level 3** (maximum) — captures the oven-spring window.
  - 15–22 min: drop to **220 °C, no steam**, vent if possible.
  - 22 min → end: switch to **Crisp Finish** at 210 °C until core probe reads **97 °C**. Total bake ~38–45 min.
- Cool on a rack at least 90 min before slicing — letting starch retrograde increases resistant starch (further GI benefit).

### Expected outcomes (with literature basis)
- **Phytate degradation: ~85–92 %.** Phytase activation soak (~40–60 %) + sourdough acidification at pH 4.5–4.6 over 14–22 h (further 30–40 percentage points on the remaining substrate) → conservatively above the user's 80 % target. Mineral (Fe, Zn, Mg) bioavailability moves from "blocked" to "good."
- **Glycemic index: ~54** (sourdough fermentation per Rolim 2024 plus rye AX and bran intactness plus cooling-induced resistant starch). Comfortably in the low band.
- **Open crumb:** moderately open (not baguette-tier — that requires white flour), with even alveoli; specific volume comparable to a well-made 100 % WW sourdough at 78 % hydration with 4 % VWG.
- **Crust:** crispy and singing; Bosch steam-3 + dry-finish + Crisp Finish produces sharp crackle.
- **Flavour:** mild lactic (lactic:acetic ~3–5), slightly nutty from rye, the hemp imperceptible. No sourness sharpness.
- **Active working time per loaf:** ~25 min total (mix 2 min + transfer 1 min + final mix 12 min + shape 1 min + score & load 2 min + tend bake transitions 5–7 min). Everything else is wall time.

## Recommendations

**Stage your adoption.** Bake V9 *as written* twice. Record final pH (Apera), bulk time, dough mass before/after bulk, oven-spring height, and slice photo. On the third bake adjust ONE variable based on data:
- If crumb is too tight → push hydration to 80 % AND drop bulk pH endpoint to 4.55.
- If loaf spreads → drop hydration to 76 % and lengthen retard to 18 h.
- If flavour is too sour → drop bulk endpoint to pH 4.7 (less acid) and confirm dough never exceeded 30 °C.
- If oven spring is poor → check levain pH was 4.2 ± 0.1 at use; if higher, build a longer levain.

**Thresholds that should change the recipe:**
- If a lab phytate assay (not realistic at home) showed <80 % degradation, extend the 45 °C soak to 60 min and add 5 g of fresh stone-ground rye to the soak.
- If you ever switch to a Dutch oven, hydration can safely go to 82 %.
- If you start eating more low-iron/low-zinc days, *do not* re-add lentil flour — instead lengthen the 45 °C soak and add 5 % more rye.

**Equipment-specific notes:**
- The Bosch HNG978NB1's core probe is the right primary doneness control; 97 °C is sufficient for whole-grain and avoids over-drying.
- The Instant Pot Pro Max sous-vide mode for the 45 °C soak is the single piece of equipment that makes V9 cleanly reproducible — without it, a yogurt maker or proofing box would be needed.
- The pH meter is for the *bulk-endpoint* call, not the levain (the levain can be eyeballed by float test and surface bubbling). Calibrate weekly.

## Caveats — where the evidence is thin or my judgment goes beyond literature

1. **The 45 °C salt-free phytase soak is well-justified mechanistically and consistent with Türk & Sandberg (1992) on wheat meal, but I am not aware of a peer-reviewed study that has measured the exact incremental phytate degradation from this specific sequence (45 °C → mix in levain → sourdough bulk) in a 100 % whole-grain free-standing loaf.** The Lean Neuentwurf's ~80 % target is extrapolated from the literature, not a directly published number. A contract-lab phytate assay would resolve this.
2. **Salt's inhibition of cereal phytase appears overstated in the popular literature.** Sedaghati et al. (2011) find slight stimulation at 0.5–1.5 % NaCl. The salt-free soak in V9 is justified by phytase pH/T optimum and bulk-mass control, not because salt would otherwise destroy the enzyme.
3. **Fermentation-temperature trade-off (25 °C best for phytate per Yıldırım 2019; 28–30 °C best for mild lactic flavour per De Vuyst 2023).** V9 places the phytate work in the *pre-fermentation soak* and lets bulk run at 28 °C for flavour. This is a recipe-design choice that resolves the conflict, not a literature consensus.
4. **Bran particle size literature is genuinely contradictory** — Noort et al. say finer is worse; Cai et al. say medium-fine is best. The user's "strong, fine whole wheat" is in the safe middle band; we should not chase ultra-fine sifted flours.
5. **Tangzhong's incompatibility with open crumb + crispy crust is a synthesis of bakery practice and primary baking-science papers, not a single controlled comparison in a sourdough hearth loaf.** A baker wanting to test could run V9 with/without a 35 g tangzhong as a side-by-side.
6. **The ~100 % phytate degradation cited for rye sourdough (Reale 2007) is from pure rye fermentation, not the 15 % rye / 85 % whole-wheat hybrid in V9.** V9 will degrade less than pure rye sourdough but materially more than pure whole-wheat sourdough — exactly the design intent.
7. **Volume penalty estimates for lentil flour are at ≥15 %; the V8 lentil dose of 15 % is at exactly the level where most studies see significant loss.** At 10 % lentil flour the penalty is much smaller, but lysine/PDCAAS still does not matter here, so the simpler omission stands.
